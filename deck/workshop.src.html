01 Introduction

How do we actually know an AI agent is good at math?

Three models are given the same problem. All three return the right answer. One derived it, one guessed a plausible number that happened to be right, and one reached it through an argument that does not hold. A scoreboard that counts final answers gives all three the same point.

An evaluation harness

The infrastructure around the model that makes an experiment repeatable, controlled, measurable, comparable and reproducible. Twelve small modules, each one shown and explained.

What we build — Part II

An LLM-as-a-judge pipeline

A rubric, a structured verdict, two judges per candidate from different model families, and an honest account of where they disagree and why that matters.

02 The experimental question

Checking the final answer is not an evaluation.

It is one bit out of a system that produced many. Here is the same claim, made concrete.

The naive evaluation

Ask the model. Take the last number. Compare it with the key. Add one to a counter. Report a percentage.

It is fast, it is cheap, and every number it produces is defensible in exactly one sense: it is reproducible from the same two strings. What it cannot tell you is anything about how the answer was obtained, what it cost to obtain, or whether the same model would obtain it again.

What we do instead

Record the whole execution: what was asked, under what configuration, what came back, what the provider exposed about its reasoning, how many tokens, how long, how much money. Then assess the reasoning separately, with a second instrument whose reliability we also measure.

Two solutions, one answer · illustration

Solution A — answer 244

Factorises the modulus condition, counts the divisor classes that satisfy it, and reduces the total mod 1000 — each step following from the last.

valid derivation

Solution B — answer 244

Computes a divisor count, asserts without argument that the remainder condition removes exactly three quarters of them, and reduces mod 1000. The arithmetic is right; the removed fraction was never established.

right answer, unsupported step

A constructed illustration, not a stored run — the real ones are in the Results section. Exact-answer scoring gives these two the same mark, and AIME answers being integers 0–999 makes the coincidence cheaper still. Everything in Part II exists because of this figure.

The four questions an evaluation should answer

1
Was the answer right?
Deterministic. Cheap. The only one most reports contain.
2
Was the reasoning valid?
Not deterministic. This is what a judge is for.
3
What did it cost?
Tokens, latency, dollars. Recorded, or it did not happen.
4
Can anyone reproduce it?
Model string, prompt hash, effort, price list, timestamp.

Notice that question 3 is free once you build the apparatus, question 1 is nearly free, question 4 is a discipline rather than a technique — and question 2 is the only genuinely hard one. Part I gets 1, 3 and 4. Part II attacks 2, and finds that the instrument needs measuring too.

    04 Part I · Harness engineering

    Everything that is not the model.

    The model is one component. It is also the component you did not write, cannot inspect, and will replace next quarter. Everything that makes a number from it trustworthy is the part you do control.

    4.1 · Concept

    What is a harness?

    Borrow the word from the laboratory. A harness is a measurement apparatus: the rig that holds the specimen, applies exactly the intended conditions, and records exactly what happened — so that the result is a property of the specimen and not of the afternoon.

    An evaluation harness is that rig for a model. It makes an experiment:

    • repeatable — the same command gives the same run
    • controlled — one variable moves at a time, and it is written down
    • measurable — tokens, latency and cost come back with the answer
    • comparable — three different APIs produce one shape of record
    • reproducible — someone else can rebuild the number from what you stored

    The model is one box in the diagram

    Nine of the ten boxes on the next page are code you write. That ratio is the point: the quality of an evaluation is almost entirely a property of the apparatus, not of the thing being evaluated.

    Diagram 1a · the harness, at the coarsest resolution

    Experiment Configuration

    Everything that decides what a run means, held in one object — because the surest way to record all of it is for all of it to be one thing that gets serialised in one place.

    ControlsWhy it is pinned
    model_keyThe exact model string on the wire. A rename must be an error, never a substitution.

    4.5 · Component 3 of 8 · the important one

    Model Adapter

    GPT, Gemini and Grok do not speak the same language. The rest of the harness must not know that.

    Diagram 2 · one interface, three dialects

    adapter.generate(prompt, reasoning_mode=HIGH)the only method the harness calls
    OpenAI adapter/v1/responses
    reasoning={"effort": "high"}
    Gemini adaptergenerate_content
    thinking_config={"thinking_budget": 16384}
    xAI adapterchat/completions
    reasoning_effort="high"
    ProviderResponseone shape, always: text · reasoning info · tokens · what was requested

    Three genuinely different requests go down; one normalised object comes back. Nothing above this line contains the word "openai" — which is why adding a fourth model is a new file, not a change to the runner, the metrics, the cost calculator or the judge.

    Why the abstraction is worth it here

    Not for elegance. For comparability. If the token accounting, the retry policy or the timeout differed per provider, a cross-model comparison would be measuring the harness as much as the models. The adapter is where those differences are forced to stop.

    One convention, enforced at the edge

    Providers disagree about whether reasoning tokens are counted inside output tokens. The adapters normalise to "yes, included" before anything downstream sees the number, so the cost calculator never has to ask which provider it is looking at — and never double-charges.

    Reasoning Configuration

    The harness has three levels. The providers have three different mechanisms — and one of them does not have all three levels at all.

    Internal vocabulary

    LOW · MEDIUM · HIGH

    One enum, used everywhere above the adapters. Nothing in the runner, the metrics or the judge knows how any provider expresses effort.

    Per-provider translation

    Each adapter maps it

    OpenAI takes the level by name. Gemini takes a token budget. xAI takes a name, but only two of them.

    When it does not exist

    unsupported

    The cell is recorded as unsupported and no call is made. It is never silently replaced by the provider default.

    Ask xAI for medium. It has no such setting. The tempting fixes are to send low, or to send nothing and let the default decide. Either produces a row labelled medium in the results table that is not a medium measurement — and no one reading the table could ever tell.

    4.7 · Component 5 of 8

    Model Execution

    What actually goes on the wire, and what comes back. One function does configure → call → time → collect → price → grade → store, and it is the whole of Part I in about sixty lines.

    It never crashes the study

    An unsupported mode, a timeout, a refusal — each becomes a stored record whose status is not ok. A grid with two failures still produces every file, and the page can draw the two holes honestly.

    It grades after the fact

    Correctness is computed from the stored response text by a checker that never touched the prompt. Extraction and grading are separate: a response with no marked answer is a formatting failure, not a wrong answer, and the two are counted separately.

    4.8 · Component 6 of 8 · the scientific caveat

    Trace Collector

    What can be collected about how the model reasoned — and, more importantly, what cannot.

    Three different things, and they must not be collapsed

    reasoning_exposure: summary

    Provider-labelled reasoning

    The API returned something it labels as reasoning — an OpenAI reasoning summary, a Gemini thought part. It is a description of the reasoning, produced by the model. It is useful. It is not the internal state.

    reasoning_exposure: token_count_only

    A number and nothing else

    We know how many reasoning tokens were billed. We know nothing whatsoever about their content. This is still information — it is what the effort comparison is built on — but it is not a trace.

    reasoning_exposure: none

    Hidden chain of thought

    Not returned, not inferable, not ours. No adapter in this repository writes model prose into the reasoning field and calls it a trace. If the API did not label it, it is response_text.

    Every stored result carries this flag, and the Results section prints it beside the run. A comparison that treats a summary as a chain of thought is not measuring what it says it is measuring.

    Do not do this

    Take the model's narration, store it as reasoning_trace, and analyse it as though it were the computation. The narration is generated alongside the answer, by the same process, with the same incentives. Treating it as evidence of the internal process is the single most common error in this area.

    Do this

    Store what the provider labelled, record which of the three cases you are in, and say so on every screen where the number appears. Then judge the written argument on its own merits — which is exactly what Part II does, and it does not need access to hidden state to do it.

    4.9 · Component 7 of 8

    Metrics Collector

    Tokens and latency, collected on every call — and the unknowns preserved as unknowns.

    null and 0 are different

    reasoning_tokens: null means "this provider did not tell us". reasoning_tokens: 0 means "it told us, and the answer was none". Collapsing them makes an average over the column silently wrong, so they stay distinct all the way to the screen, which prints n/a for one and 0 for the other.

    Why token usage matters here specifically

    Reasoning mode is the independent variable of this study, and reasoning tokens are the most direct evidence that changing it did anything at all. If high and low consume the same tokens, either the mode was not applied or the provider ignored it — and the accuracy comparison you were about to publish is between two identical arms.

    Wall clock, not the provider's timing

    What the experiment is comparing is what a user would wait for, queueing and all.

    {{CODE|harness/metrics.py|usage_block|the unknowns survive as null}} {{CODE|harness/metrics.py|stopwatch|the latency is a latency that elapsed}}

    4.10 · Component 8 of 8

    Cost Calculator

    The whole formula

    cost = input_tokens   x input_price
     + output_tokens  x output_price
     + reasoning_tokens x reasoning_price

where reasoning_price defaults to output_price,
because most providers bill it that way — but it
is a separate field, not an assumption.

    One price list, versioned

    Prices change. A price written into the module that calls the API means that six months from now the results table is wrong and nobody can tell. Every price lives in one dict, each row carries the date it was checked, and PRICING_VERSION travels into every stored result. A cost is only reproducible if you know which price list produced it.

    Itemised, not one float

    Returned as a breakdown so the page can show where the money went. At high effort the reasoning tokens are usually most of it, and that is the point the cost section is making.

    To update prices: edit harness/pricing.py, bump PRICING_VERSION, re-run analysis.build_results. Nothing else changes.

    07 Part II · LLM-as-a-judge

    We now have twenty-four solutions. Which one is better?

    The harness answered three of the four questions. It cannot answer the fourth, because there is no deterministic check for "is this argument valid". So we reach for a second instrument — and immediately acquire the problem of validating it.

    7.1 · Concept

    What is LLM-as-a-judge?

    Another language model reads the candidate solution and scores it against a predefined rubric. That is the whole idea. Everything difficult about it is in the words predefined rubric and in what you do with the score afterwards.

    Why we need it at all

    Both solutions on the right answer 42. The answer key marks both correct. No amount of string normalisation will ever separate them, because the difference is not in the answer — it is in the argument.

    What it is not

    It is not ground truth. It is a measuring instrument with known biases, run on a model that will confidently score mathematics it has misread. Part II spends as much time on that as on the pipeline itself.

    Do not ask this

    "Is this answer good?" — a single number that means whatever the model felt like, on a scale it invented, with no way to locate a disagreement.

    The case the judge exists for

    Both answer 42 · answer key says 42exact-answer scoring: 1 point each
    A

    Derives n = 21p, argues p = 2, verifies 8 divisors.

    B

    "42 feels right for a divisor-counting problem."

    Rubric-scored by two judgesreasoning 5/5 vs 1–3/5 · verdict pass vs fail

    The judge does not improve the accuracy number. It adds a second axis the accuracy number was hiding.

    7.3 · Component 1

    Candidate Output

    Exactly what is being evaluated, and — just as importantly — what is withheld.

    The judge sees

    • The problem statementas the solver saw it
    • The full solution textthe model's written argument
    • The answer it claimsas extracted by the harness
    • The rubricwith written anchors

    The judge does not see

    • The expected answerit must do the mathematics itself
    • The harness's correctness verdictthe outcome would leak into every other criterion
    • Which model wrote itno brand to prefer
    • The reasoning mode, tokens or cost"this is the expensive high-effort run" is not evidence

    Why withhold the answer key?

    It costs accuracy on criterion 1 and buys the other five. A judge told the answer was right rationalises the route that reached it; a judge told it was wrong finds faults in reasoning that is sound. The harness already knows the truth deterministically, so handing it over purchases nothing.

    And it turns into a measurement

    Because the judge scores correctness blind, the gap between its correctness score and the deterministic check becomes a real measurement of the judge. That number is reported in the Results section.

    7.4 · Component 2

    Judge Prompt

    One function assembles everything the judge is shown, so "what was the judge asked?" has a single answer that fits on a slide.

    Standardised, or nothing is comparable

    Two judges scoring against two slightly different prompts are two different instruments, and their agreement is meaningless. The prompt is built by one function, hashed into every verdict record, and identical across all four judge configurations.

    Assembled, not concatenated at the call site

    Every piece of the prompt is a named block. If someone adds a sentence, the hash moves and every downstream verdict is visibly from a different apparatus.

    7.5 · Component 3

    The Rubric

    Six criteria with written anchors, so that a 3 means the same thing to two different judges.

    A vibe

    "Rate this solution from 1 to 10." Nothing anchors 7. Two judges will not mean the same thing by it, and when they differ you cannot say about what.

    A rubric

    Six named criteria, each with a written description of what 5, 3 and 1 look like, plus an ordered error-severity scale. When two judges differ you can point at the criterion and read both anchors aloud.

    Anchors are the difference. Without them a rubric is six vibes instead of one.

    7.6 · Component 4

    Structured Output

    The judge returns one JSON object. A judge that writes prose is a judge whose scores must be read by a human — which is the cost the whole method exists to avoid.

    Parsing is a first-class outcome

    A judge that returns something else has not returned a low score; it has returned nothing. That is stored as status: "parse_failed" with the raw text kept, and it is counted on this page. A pipeline that silently drops unparseable verdicts reports the agreement rate of the cases the judges found easy to format.

    Validated, not trusted

    Tolerant about packaging — a fenced block or an object embedded in commentary is accepted, because that is a formatting quirk. Strict about content: every criterion present, integers in 1–5, severity and verdict from the rubric's own lists. 4.5 is rejected: a judge answering off a five-point scale has not used the rubric.

    What comes back

  "correctness": 5,
  "reasoning": 3,

  "efficiency": 4,
  "clarity": 3,
  "error_severity": "major",

    Why not one judge?

    A single judge gives you a number with no error bar and no way to notice it is wrong. Two judges from different families give you a disagreement rate — which is the cheapest available evidence about how much to trust either of them.

    Why not five?

    Cost. Judging is already the larger half of this study's bill. Two is the smallest number that produces the diagnostic, and the diagnostic is what matters.

    7.8 · Component 6

    Judge Reasoning Modes

    If reasoning effort changes how a model solves, does it change how a model judges?

    Every candidate is judged twice by each judge — once at low effort and once at high. Those are the two levels all three providers expose, which keeps the judge sweep even; the medium hole in the solver grid would otherwise reappear here and make the comparison uneven.

    The question this makes answerable

    Does a more expensive judge produce a better verdict, or merely a longer one? Compare the same judge on the same solution at two efforts: the score, the verdict, the explanation, the tokens, the latency and the cost are all recorded for both.

    Why it matters practically

    Judging cost scales with candidates × judges × judge efforts. If low-effort judging agrees with high-effort judging, the evaluation budget drops by most of itself. If it does not, you have found a reason to be careful about every published judge score that did not report its effort setting.

    Four verdicts per candidate

    JudgeEffortWhat we compare
    Judge Alowscore per criterion · verdict · explanation · tokens · latency · cost
    Judge Ahigh
    Judge Blow
    Judge Bhigh

    24 candidates × 2 judges × 2 efforts = 96 verdicts. The Results section lets you hold three of those four dimensions still and move the fourth.

    The question to put to the room

    Two strong models read the same reasoning trace and score it 5 and 3. Why?

    There is no single answer, and the plausible ones are all uncomfortable: the rubric anchor is ambiguous; one judge missed a step; one judge is systematically more generous; the solution is genuinely borderline; or one of them is simply wrong about the mathematics.     7.10

    Advantages and limitations.

    Both lists, stated in full, because a method presented without its failure modes is being sold rather than taught.

    Advantages

    • ScalableNinety-six verdicts in minutes. Human annotation of the same corpus is days.
    • InexpensiveCents per verdict against tens of dollars per hour of expert time.
    • FlexibleA new criterion is a line in a dict, applied to a corpus you already paid to produce.
    • Handles open-ended reasoningWhere no deterministic check exists, this is the only automated option.
    • Explains itselfEvery score comes with prose you can read and argue with — unlike a similarity metric.
    • AutomatableStructured output drops straight into a pipeline, a dashboard or a regression test.

    Limitations

    • Judge biasSystematic leniency or severity that shifts every score in one direction.
    • Model-family preferenceJudges favour output that reads like their own. Excluded here by rotation, not measured.
    • Verbosity biasLonger solutions score higher for looking more thorough.
    • Position biasIn pairwise setups, the option shown first wins more often than it should.
    • Self-preferenceA model scores its own output highest. Structurally impossible in this design.
    • Prompt sensitivityRewording the rubric moves the scores. The prompt hash is stored for exactly this reason.
    • Fails on hard mathematicsA judge that cannot solve the problem cannot reliably grade a solution to it. This is the ceiling.
    • Correlated errorsTwo judges trained on overlapping data can be wrong together, and agreement will not reveal it.
    • DisagreementTwo judges, two answers, and no principled tie-break.
    • Cost multipliesTwo judges at two efforts is four calls per candidate — usually more than the solving cost.
    • Reasoning-mode dependenceThe same judge at a different effort is a different instrument.

    The sentence to say out loud

    LLM-as-a-judge is an evaluation tool, not ground truth.

    Which is why every judge number on this page is shown next to the deterministic correctness check, the inter-judge agreement, and the count of verdicts that failed to parse. A judge score without those three is a number with no error bar.

    10 Reproducibility

    What has to be recorded for a number to mean anything.

    1. Fixed prompts — stored, and hashed into every record.
    2. Fixed problem versions — problem_set_version travels with each run.
    3. Explicit model versions — the exact string on the wire, never an alias.
    4. Explicit reasoning settings — and the literal request that was sent, not just the label.
    5. Timestamps — UTC, per run.
    6. API configuration — endpoint, token cap, retry policy, attempts used.
    7. Stored raw responses — the full text, so a new checker can be applied later.
    8. Token counts — with unknowns preserved as null.
    9. Pricing version — because a dollar figure without a price list is not a figure.
    10. Deterministic evaluation where possible — judged only where no check exists.

    11 Conclusions

    Evaluating an AI agent means evaluating the system around it.

    1. A final answer is one bit. Two solutions reaching 42 are not the same result, and no string comparison will ever separate them.
    2. The apparatus is most of the work. Nine of the ten boxes in the architecture are code you write. The quality of an evaluation is almost entirely a property of those nine.
    3. Abstraction buys comparability, not elegance. One interface over three APIs is what stops a cross-model comparison from measuring the harness.
    4. Say unsupported. A capability a provider does not have must be a hole in the grid, never a substituted default.
    5. Do not fabricate reasoning. Provider-labelled summary, token count, and inaccessible hidden state are three different things.
    1. A judge needs a rubric with anchors. Otherwise you have six vibes instead of one.
    2. Structured output, and parse failures counted. Silently dropping unparseable verdicts reports the agreement rate of the easy cases.
    3. Two judges, never the candidate's own family. Exclude self-preference by design rather than measuring it and hoping.
    4. Verdict agreement is not agreement. Report per-criterion difference, or you will call a three-point gap perfect consensus.
    5. The judge is an instrument, not ground truth. Report its agreement with the deterministic check, its severity profile, and what it cost.

    One line to leave with

    If you cannot say which model string, which prompt, which reasoning setting, which price list and which judge produced a number — you do not have a measurement. You have an anecdote with a decimal point.
